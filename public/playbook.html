How to follow up quotes without being pushy.

A short routine for plumbers, electricians, builders, decorators and other tradespeople who want fewer forgotten quotes—not more awkward sales messages.

Use the free boardAsk about setup

1. Choose the next date before you move on

When you send a quote, decide when you will check in. A simple starting point is 1–2 days after sending, then again around day 5–7 if there is no answer. The right timing depends on the job and what you agreed with the customer.

2. Send something useful and human

Keep the message short. Ask whether the quote arrived, offer to clarify the scope, or check whether the customer wants to change an option. Avoid pretending there is a deadline or scarcity when there is not.

After 1–2 days

Hi — just checking you received the quote for [job]. Happy to answer any questions or clarify the options.

After 5–7 days

Hi [name], I’m planning the next few weeks and wanted to check whether you’d like me to hold a slot for [job]. No pressure if plans have changed.

After 14 days

Hi [name], I’ll close this quote for now so I don’t keep bothering you. If you’d like to revisit [job] later, just reply and I’ll pick it back up.

3. Record the outcome

Every follow-up should end in a next action: won, lost, paused, or a new date. This is how you learn whether the issue is timing, price, scope, trust or simply that the customer changed plans.

4. Protect the customer relationship

  • Edit every template so it sounds like you.
  • Stop if a customer says no or asks not to be contacted.
  • Do not invent reviews, qualifications, urgency, prices or guarantees.
  • Keep a note of what was discussed so the next conversation is useful.

5. Review once a week

Look at open quotes, follow-ups completed, replies, won/lost outcomes and quoted value. The goal is not maximum messages; it is fewer forgotten opportunities and a better customer experience.
